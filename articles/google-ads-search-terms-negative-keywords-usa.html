US Google Ads Search Quality

Google Ads search terms and negative keywords for US lead generation

Updated September 30, 2026. Search-term review is a recurring business-quality check, not a one-time attempt to build the longest possible exclusion list.

Keywords and search terms are different evidence

A keyword is a targeting input. A search term is the query Google reports after a person searched and an ad was eligible to show. The search terms report helps explain how targeting, match behavior, language, location settings, and user intent combined in the auction. It does not expose every query because reporting thresholds and privacy protections apply.

For US lead generation, the useful question is not simply whether a query contains the service name. Ask whether the person appears to need the service, whether the business can serve the location and use case, and whether the resulting inquiry became qualified.

Build a review sheet around decisions

  • Query, campaign, ad group, keyword, and match type.
  • Impressions, clicks, cost, primary conversions, and conversion value where appropriate.
  • Landing page and advertised service.
  • Lead status: valid, duplicate, spam, job seeker, vendor, student, existing customer, or qualified opportunity.
  • Decision: keep, promote to a keyword, exclude, route elsewhere, or collect more evidence.

Use enough history to avoid reacting to one click, but review new campaigns more frequently while query patterns are still forming. Weekly triage and a deeper monthly review are often more useful than daily bulk exclusions.

Classify intent before adding a negative

Common noncommercial themes include jobs, salaries, training, certification, free downloads, definitions, login and support requests, suppliers, and unrelated locations. These are examples for classification, not a universal negative list. A training provider should not exclude training terms; a software company may want documentation searches; a national provider should not remove every city outside its headquarters.

Review modifiers in context. A phrase such as “cost,” “reviews,” or “near me” can signal commercial research. Excluding it without checking the offer and sales process may remove useful demand.

Choose the negative match deliberately

Negative broad, phrase, and exact match do not behave exactly like their positive-keyword counterparts. Before adding an exclusion, test the words and word order it may block. Use account-level or shared exclusions only when the theme is genuinely irrelevant across the affected campaigns. Keep campaign-specific conflicts local.

  • Use exact exclusions for a narrow unwanted query when related variants may still be valuable.
  • Use phrase exclusions when the complete phrase consistently expresses an irrelevant intent.
  • Use broader exclusions only after checking plural forms, synonyms, reordered words, and legitimate service combinations.
  • Record the reason and date so future reviewers can reverse a bad decision.

Connect query optimization to lead quality

Platform conversions alone can reward poor inquiries. Import qualified-lead or opportunity outcomes where the business has consent, stable identifiers, and an appropriate CRM process. Compare query themes with downstream quality rather than optimizing only for form completion.

If a query generates valid but low-close-rate leads, investigate geography, offer clarity, price expectations, sales response time, and landing-page qualification before excluding the theme. Search terms reveal demand, but they do not prove why a lead failed.

Guardrails for broad match and automation

Broader matching can discover demand, but it needs reliable conversion definitions, sufficient feedback, budget controls, and search-term monitoring. Do not expand because the platform recommends it without confirming what the campaign is optimizing. Separate brand and non-brand reporting when that distinction affects decisions, and protect campaigns from internal competition through clear structure rather than excessive exclusions.

A repeatable 30-minute review

  1. Sort recent queries by spend, conversions, and emerging volume.
  2. Label business relevance and lead quality using agreed definitions.
  3. Investigate expensive ambiguous terms before changing them.
  4. Add only reviewed exclusions at the narrowest appropriate scope.
  5. Promote proven themes into clearer ad groups or landing-page tests.
  6. Log changes and review their effect after enough new evidence accumulates.
